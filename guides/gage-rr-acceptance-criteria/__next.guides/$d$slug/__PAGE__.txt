<h1 style="font-size:29px;line-height:1.25;margin:14px 0 6px;">Gage R&amp;R: what the 10% / 30% thresholds really mean (and what they don't)</h1>
<p style="color:#5b6675;font-size:15px;margin:0 0 6px;">%GRR, ndc, %Tolerance, ANOVA vs X̄-R — and why a study that passed can still be useless.</p>
<p style="color:#8a94a3;font-size:12px;margin:0 0 16px;">Updated 13 November 2026 · 7 min read · Written for working labs and quality teams</p>
<div style="background:#eef4ff;border-left:4px solid #1e4d8b;border-radius:8px;padding:14px 18px;margin:0 0 22px;"><p style="margin:0 0 6px;font-size:11px;font-weight:700;letter-spacing:.6px;color:#1e4d8b;text-transform:uppercase;">Short answer</p><p style="margin:0;font-size:15px;">%GRR is the share of observed variation attributable to the measurement system. The AIAG MSA reference manual's conventional bands are <b>under 10% acceptable</b>, <b>10–30% potentially acceptable</b> depending on the application, cost and customer, and <b>over 30% needing improvement</b>. These are industry conventions, not ISO clauses. Read them together with ndc (number of distinct categories, commonly ≥ 5), %Tolerance, and whether the parts in the study actually spanned the process variation.</p></div>
<div style="border:1px solid #e6e9ef;border-radius:8px;padding:12px 16px;margin:0 0 24px;"><p style="margin:0 0 6px;font-size:12px;font-weight:700;color:#5b6675;">On this page</p><ol style="margin:0;padding-left:20px;font-size:13.5px;"><li><a href="#s1" style="color:#2563eb;">The numbers you get from a study</a></li><li><a href="#s2" style="color:#2563eb;">A result is not a verdict on its own</a></li><li><a href="#s3" style="color:#2563eb;">What to do when the result is marginal</a></li><li><a href="#where-it-goes-wrong" style="color:#2563eb;">Where this goes wrong in practice</a></li><li><a href="#faq" style="color:#2563eb;">Frequently asked</a></li></ol></div>
<h2 id="s1" style="font-size:20px;margin-top:30px;margin-bottom:8px;">The numbers you get from a study</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 12px;">The classic design is <b>10 parts × 3 operators × 3 trials</b>, randomised, with parts selected to span the normal process range. ANOVA is preferred over the older X̄-R (range) method because it estimates the operator-by-part interaction instead of folding it into the residual.</p>

<h2 id="s2" style="font-size:20px;margin-top:30px;margin-bottom:8px;">A result is not a verdict on its own</h2>
<ul style="margin:0 0 14px;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin-bottom:7px;"><b>A study can pass with the wrong parts.</b> If the ten parts are all from one tight batch, process variation is understated and %GRR looks worse; if they are hand-picked across a far wider range than production, %GRR looks better than reality. Select parts to represent the process, not to make the number move.</li><li style="margin-bottom:7px;"><b>%GRR and %Tolerance answer different questions.</b> A wide tolerance can give a comfortable %Tolerance while %GRR against process variation is poor — that matters when the chart is used to detect small shifts.</li><li style="margin-bottom:7px;"><b>Repeatability vs reproducibility tells you where to work.</b> High EV points at the gage, fixturing or resolution; high AV points at operator technique, training or a poorly defined measurement method; a significant operator × part interaction usually means the method is being applied differently depending on the part.</li><li style="margin-bottom:7px;"><b>GR&amp;R is one study, not the whole MSA.</b> Bias, linearity, stability and (for attribute gages) agreement studies are separate. A destructive test, a chemical assay or a vision system needs a different design altogether.</li></ul>
<p style="background:#fff8e8;border:1px solid #f0d9a0;border-radius:8px;padding:11px 15px;margin:0 0 16px;font-size:14px;"><b>Practical note.</b> Resolution still matters: a gage that reads in 0.1 mm increments cannot convincingly support a study on a 0.05 mm spread. The old "10:1 rule of thumb" has been replaced by more nuanced guidance in current MSA references — check the resolution and the number of distinct categories actually obtained rather than quoting a ratio from memory.</p>
<h2 id="s3" style="font-size:20px;margin-top:30px;margin-bottom:8px;">What to do when the result is marginal</h2>
<ol style="margin:0 0 14px;padding-left:22px;"><li style="margin-bottom:9px;">Check the study design first: part selection, randomisation, blind measurement, and whether operators followed the same defined method.</li><li style="margin-bottom:9px;">Look at the ANOVA table to see whether the problem is equipment or operator — the fix is completely different.</li><li style="margin-bottom:9px;">If 10–30% is genuinely conditional, document the justification: characteristic criticality, customer agreement, cost of improvement, and what additional controls (e.g. averaging repeat readings) are used.</li><li style="margin-bottom:9px;">After any change to the gage, fixture or method, re-run the study. A MSA result belongs to a specific system, not to the part number forever.</li></ol>
<h2 id="where-it-goes-wrong" style="font-size:20px;margin-top:30px;margin-bottom:8px;">Where this goes wrong in practice</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 11px;">Treating 10% and 30% as pass/fail requirements in a standard. They are widely used AIAG MSA conventions; customer-specific requirements may set different thresholds.</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 11px;">Selecting ten near-identical parts, which inflates %GRR and triggers an unnecessary gage replacement project.</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 11px;">Reporting %Tolerance when the customer asked for %GRR against total variation (or the reverse) without saying which denominator was used.</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 11px;">Letting operators watch each other's readings, or measuring in part order instead of randomised order — both quietly destroy the study's independence.</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 11px;">Running one study and never repeating it after a fixture change, a new operator group or a gage repair.</p>
<h2 id="faq" style="font-size:20px;margin-top:30px;margin-bottom:8px;">Frequently asked</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 10px;"><b>Are the 10% / 30% thresholds in ISO 9001 or IATF 16949?</b><br>No. They come from the AIAG MSA reference manual (and equivalent customer references) and are applied through customer-specific requirements, PPAP submissions and control plans. The standards require suitable measurement resources and valid results; the numeric bands are conventional.</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 10px;"><b>What is ndc and why does it matter?</b><br>Number of distinct categories (also called number of distinct data classes) estimates how many separate groups the measurement system can reliably distinguish within the observed variation. A value below 2 means the system cannot tell products apart; ≥ 5 is the commonly expected minimum for a usable process-control gage.</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 10px;"><b>Can I average multiple readings instead of improving the gage?</b><br>Sometimes — averaging repeated measurements reduces the repeatability component, but it must be defined in the measurement method, applied consistently, and reflected in the control plan. It does not fix a reproducibility problem or poor resolution.</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 10px;"><b>How often should a Gage R&amp;R study be repeated?</b><br>There is no universal interval. Re-run it when the gage, fixture, method, operator population or characteristic changes, when a customer requires it, and at the frequency your control plan or customer-specific requirements define.</p>
